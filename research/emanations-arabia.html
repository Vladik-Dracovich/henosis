Ancient Arabian inscriptions

← BOOK OF EMANATIONS

TEXTUAL AND THEOLOGICAL COMMENTARY

Old South Arabian scripts

Ancient South Arabian monumental writing generally uses a consonantal alphabet with distinctive letter forms. Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic and Hadramitic are linguistic categories, not merely alternative fonts.

A modern Arabic sentence written with musnad characters is not thereby an ancient South Arabian inscription. Identify language, grammar and attested vocabulary independently of script appearance.

Sabaic religion

Sabaic dedications often record donors, divine recipients and the circumstances of offerings. Monumental inscriptions can also document political authority and collective obligations.

Read a dedication in relation to its sanctuary and dating evidence. The formulaic nature of epigraphic language limits what can be inferred about private mystical experience or a comprehensive theology.

Almaqah

Almaqah's cult is prominently attested at the Awwam sanctuary near Marib. His titles and associations belong to the institutional history of Saba.

Astral identifications proposed in older scholarship should be tested against the actual epithets and iconography. A crescent motif alone cannot establish a complete lunar theology.

Athtar

Athtar is attested across several South Arabian kingdoms and can appear with locally differentiated epithets. His cult is not exhausted by a single astronomical identification.

Compare dedications across linguistic and regional corpora, keeping the epithet attached to its witness. A modern invocation may synthesize these titles only if it identifies the synthesis as new.

Minaic inscriptions

Minaic inscriptions attest communities connected with long-distance trade, including evidence outside the South Arabian homeland. Dedications illuminate collective identity and religious institutions.

Distinguish the movement of merchants and writing practices from proof of a uniform cult across every trading settlement. Provenance and chronology are essential to the comparison.

Qatabanic traditions

The god Amm is prominent in Qatabanic inscriptions and is associated with the political and communal identity of Qataban. Other deities and institutions appear alongside him.

The divine name's linguistic associations cannot alone establish a complete genealogy or cosmology. Read royal and dedicatory formulas in their specific institutional settings.

Hadramitic traditions

Sayin is prominently attested in Hadramitic religious material, including inscriptions connected with the kingdom's cultic centers. Regional evidence complicates a generalized South Arabian pantheon.

Compare Hadramitic formulas with neighboring corpora without automatically transferring Sabaic divine titles. The local language and sanctuary context are part of the evidence.

Safaitic inscriptions

Safaitic inscriptions were carved across the basalt deserts of northern Arabia and adjacent regions. Their short texts often record movement, grief, genealogies and appeals to gods.

These personal inscriptions offer a different evidentiary perspective from monumental royal dedications. Consult the OCIANA corpus for readings and provenance before generalizing about nomadic religious life.

Al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat

Al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat are known from evidence of different regions and periods, including inscriptions and later Arabic literary sources. Their cultic associations are not uniform.

Distinguish contemporary epigraphic attestations from retrospective accounts. Similar names across languages and regions require historical argument before being assigned a single identity or ritual system.

Epigraphic reconstruction

Epigraphic editions use conventions to distinguish visible letters, damaged traces, restorations and uncertain readings. A photograph or drawing may preserve evidence not obvious in a normalized transcription.

Record corpus identifier, findspot, language, edition and proposed dating. A reconstructed devotional passage should never be presented as an ancient inscription unless its wording is actually attested.

PRIMARY TEXTS

Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic, Hadramitic, Safaitic and Hismaic inscriptions; dedications, invocations and funerary texts.

RESEARCH DIRECTION

Study attested divine names including Athtar, Almaqah, Wadd, Sayin, al-Lat and other regionally documented deities. Avoid treating distinct languages and local cults as a unified pantheon.

TEXTUAL METHOD

Record the edition, manuscript or inscription identifier, passage number, original language, transliteration and translation before making comparative claims. Mark damaged readings, uncertain restorations and modern adaptations explicitly.

EDITIONS AND FURTHER READING

Ahmad Al-Jallad, The Religion and Rituals of the Nomads of Pre-Islamic Arabia (2022); inscription records in DASI and OCIANA.
